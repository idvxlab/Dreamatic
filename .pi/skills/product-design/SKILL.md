# Product Design

Use this optional module for industrial, consumer, furniture, appliance, mobility, wearable, or hardware concepts. It supports professional product reasoning without requiring a fixed deliverable set.

## Design Frame

Connect the concept across these dimensions:

- target user, use context, unmet need, and desired experience;
- primary and secondary functions, controls, feedback, and interaction flow;
- ergonomics, reach, grip, posture, accessibility, safety, and human scale;
- product architecture, part relationships, interfaces, service access, and assembly intent;
- form semantics, proportion, silhouette, visual center, and brand character;
- color, material, finish, tactility, durability, cleaning, and aging;
- manufacturing plausibility, repair, packaging, transport, and product lifecycle.

Do not claim technical feasibility, compliance, or performance without evidence. Mark unresolved engineering matters as assumptions or validation needs.

## Reference Use

First identify the task: new product, authorized redesign, faithful depiction
of an existing product, or product communication. For a new product, extract
functional principles, interaction patterns, ergonomics, construction logic,
CMF behavior and category expectations, then develop an independently reasoned
architecture and form. Do not substitute a competitor's distinctive design
with only a new color or mark. Explain where the new solution differs and why.
For depiction or communication of the existing product, preserving identifying
features may be essential; do not redesign it to satisfy a generic originality
rule. For redesign, distinguish retained equity from authorized changes.
Scope usage conditions to the actual asset and intended operation; studying
category mechanisms is not permission to reuse a source image or existing mark.

## Proposal Coverage

Select distinct views that comprehensively explain the product. The brief and design questions determine image count. Depending on the brief, this may include:

- a hero view that communicates the concept and intended context;
- multi-view or orthographic-style views for proportion and continuity;
- an in-use view for ergonomics and interaction;
- a detail or CMF study for critical interfaces and material transitions;
- an exploded or architecture view only when internal relationships matter;
- variants when a family, different use conditions or comparison improves the proposal.

Each planned image should specify purpose, viewpoint, visible features, consistency anchors, acceptance criteria, aspect ratio, and size. Use the lowest resolution that still communicates the required evidence.
Use these categories as starting points, not a six-image template or ceiling.
Develop relevant alternative mechanisms/forms, multi-angle views, distinct
users/scenarios, operating states, assembly/service relationships and component
details into their own views where useful. A hero image cannot substitute for
those conclusions merely because the product looks attractive.

## From Need to Product

Separate the user outcome from an assumed product format. Build a morphological
map across applicable functional architecture, control/use sequence, support,
storage, form and material; vary important axes to create distinct concepts.
Use reversal, integration/separation or an adjacent-domain mechanism to challenge
category conventions, then explain the resulting user benefit. Reject combinations
that contradict operating conditions rather than arbitrary unfamiliar forms.
Compare use, ergonomics, coherence, differentiation, plausible assembly and
lifecycle/cost drivers. Develop selected relationships through sections, contact
zones and assembly logic, not only a hero silhouette. Record assumptions needing
a mockup, fit test, material sample or engineering review.

Translate the Researcher's findings into a function hierarchy: primary user
outcome, supporting tasks and constraints. Explore alternatives in use and
architecture before selecting a concept; do not reduce product design to a
styled enclosure. Explain the proposed interaction through preparation, use,
feedback, cleaning, storage and maintenance where applicable.

Specify the relationships between controls, product states and perceived
affordances. State critical human-scale assumptions and the observations or
prototypes needed to validate them. Use the canonical Design Spec to retain
decisions, unresolved engineering questions and the purpose of each view.

This module is independently usable. Industrial manufacturing guidance can
deepen a production-focused brief, but is not a prerequisite for a product
concept. Generated views communicate design intent rather than validate
engineering, assembly tolerances or manufacturing readiness.

## Quality Lens

A strong product concept is coherent from user need to function, interaction, form, and CMF. Novelty should improve meaning or use rather than add arbitrary complexity.
